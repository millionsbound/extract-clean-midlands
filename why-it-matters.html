Why extract cleaning matters

Grease in a kitchen extract system is a fire hazard. UK fire safety law requires the risk to be assessed and reduced. Insurers may also ask for proof that the system has been cleaned to a recognised standard.

Grease in the ducts can spread a fire

When a commercial kitchen is cooking, the extract system draws grease, oil vapours and smoke into the canopy and along the ductwork. Some of that grease is caught by the filters. None of the filters is complete, so deposits still form on the hidden surfaces behind and beyond the canopy.

London Fire Brigade's guidance on catering kitchen extract systems puts it plainly: all internal surfaces of these systems are affected by grease and oil deposits, and no filter is 100% effective. Its Fire Investigation Team says that "by far the most common problem and cause of ducting fires is the lack of proper cleaning and maintenance."

BESA, which publishes TR19 Grease, says fires in which commercial kitchen extract systems were a source or a contributory factor have focused the attention of insurers, property owners and landlords on correct cleaning and maintenance. If grease in the duct ignites, fire can travel through the system and into other parts of the building.

The law: a responsible person must assess the risk

The Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005 applies to non-domestic premises in England and Wales, including restaurants and takeaways. The "responsible person" is usually the employer, or the person who has control of the premises in connection with the business.

Article 9 of the Order requires that person to make a suitable and sufficient assessment of the fire risks to which people are exposed, and to identify the general fire precautions they need to take. Grease build-up in a kitchen extract system is a fire hazard, so it belongs in that assessment, with a cleaning regime that actually keeps the risk down.

BESA and London Fire Brigade both treat regular inspection and cleaning of grease extract systems as part of meeting that duty. If there is a fire, investigators often ask for evidence that the system was maintained in line with a recognised standard such as TR19.

What insurers may ask for

We do not claim that every policy says the same thing, and we cannot say what your own insurer will do. The official and industry sources are more careful than that, and so are we.

BESA says TR19 Grease was produced in response to increasing requirements from insurers, property owners and regulatory bodies, and that it supports insurance expectations for commercial premises. In its guidance on kitchen extract cleaning, BESA also says many providers now require proof that kitchen extract ductwork is regularly cleaned and inspected, and that failure to follow a recognised standard may lead insurers to deny a claim if they trace a fire back to poor maintenance.

London Fire Brigade makes a similar point: any failure to observe a proper cleaning and maintenance regime could affect a subsequent insurance claim.

What you can usefully keep is a dated post-clean report, with photographs and, where they were taken, deposit test results. That is the record a landlord, fire officer or insurer can ask to see.

Hygiene as well as fire

BESA also notes that accumulated grease can cause foul odours, pest problems and, in some cases, unhygienic air being pushed back into the cooking area. That is a separate reason to keep the system clean, alongside the fire risk.

If you have been asked for a certificate

If a landlord, insurer or fire risk assessor has asked for proof of a recent extract clean, say so on the enquiry form. We will pass that on so the firm can quote for a documented TR19-style clean, not just a canopy wipe.
